I’ve been thinking a great deal about how we educate engineers, particularly as I’ve been advising Indiana Tech on its curriculum.

Over the years, I’ve heard something repeatedly from our interns: they often feel they learn more during an internship than they do in the classroom.

That observation has bothered me. Why should a few months working on real projects be more educational than years spent attending lectures? And what would happen if we redesigned engineering education around the experience that makes internships so valuable?

I believe we should start by working backward from the outcome we want: a graduate who can independently learn, solve difficult technical problems, collaborate with others, communicate clearly, and actually build things that work.

My vision is to make the university operate more like an ecosystem of small engineering companies.

Each semester, students would form multidisciplinary teams, take ownership of real engineering challenges, and work toward demonstrable outcomes. Professors would function more like technical directors, mentors, project managers, and CEOs of small ventures than traditional lecturers. Students themselves would rotate through leadership responsibilities.

We already have extraordinary educational resources available through 3Blue1Brown, Crash Course, Floating Head Physics, Brilliant, and countless other sources. Rather than having faculty repeatedly deliver lectures that may already exist in excellent form, why not curate the best explanations and dedicate more faculty time to helping students understand, experiment, design, and apply that knowledge?

This would not mean abandoning mathematics, physics, or engineering fundamentals. Quite the opposite. Students would be expected to demonstrate mastery by applying those subjects to real problems.

I also want students learning how professional engineering teams actually work.

Teams should practice Agile development and Scrum, maintaining backlogs, planning sprints, holding regular standups, reviewing deliverables, and reflecting on what they learned.

I’ve been developing Pulse, an open-source short-form video platform that I envision students using to record brief Beats or updates during standups. Every student should regularly explain what they accomplished, what they’re working on, what they’ve learned, and where they’re stuck.

Communication shouldn’t be relegated to a public-speaking class. It should become a daily engineering discipline. Explaining a problem clearly is itself evidence of how well you understand it.

We already have an example of this approach in our MIE open-source internship projects. Interns are invited to contribute to actual engineering projects, work with existing codebases, diagnose problems, document their decisions, and deliver changes that others can use.

My surprise came when I started studying ABET accreditation.

I initially worried that accreditation would be an obstacle to this kind of educational transformation. I assumed it might prescribe traditional courses, lectures, and academic structures.

Instead, I discovered remarkable harmony between ABET’s engineering student outcomes and what I’m proposing.

ABET explicitly emphasizes engineering problem-solving, design, effective communication, professional responsibility, teamwork, experimentation, and the ability to acquire new knowledge independently.

Even more interesting, ABET generally specifies what engineering students must learn without prescribing the specific instructional methods universities must use. Programs still need to satisfy technical content, credit-hour, faculty, and assessment requirements, but there is meaningful freedom in how they deliver education.

In other words, ABET might actually provide a framework for measuring whether this educational model works rather than standing in its way.

That realization has encouraged me to take the idea further.

I’ve started a GitHub project:

Engineering Education: Working Backward from the Ideal Graduate

My intention is to collaboratively develop an ideal engineering curriculum, starting with the competencies we want graduates to possess and working backward to the educational experiences, projects, resources, faculty roles, and assessments necessary to produce them.

I want to explore what a four-year program might look like if students spent much of their time practicing engineering, supported by the best educational resources available, rather than primarily listening to lectures.

I also want the proposal to be rigorous. We should be able to map it to ABET requirements, measure individual competency, evaluate outcomes, and compare it objectively with conventional educational approaches.

I’m not suggesting that I have all the answers. I do believe we should be willing to question assumptions about how universities operate, especially when technology is changing how knowledge is acquired and how engineering gets done.

I’d welcome participation from Indiana Tech’s faculty and leadership, practicing engineers, employers, students, and anyone interested in rethinking engineering education.

What if students graduated not just with a degree, but with four years of experience actually doing engineering?

That’s the outcome I’d like us to work backward from.
